# Eco-communities and eco-projects that align with Terrarium

**Pass:** eco-villages, intentional communities, cohousing, suburban retrofit, agrihoods, Transition initiatives, permaculture institutes, and resilience educators.  
**Date of this sweep:** 11 November 2026, America/Los_Angeles.  
**Status:** research only. Nobody was contacted. Luke sends any outreach himself. Do not treat this file as a live partnership list.

## How to read this file

Terrarium is Luke Spooner's neighborhood-resilience project: the Suburban Gap thesis; 12 capability domains; community "stations" of about 50–150 people grown from existing suburbia that split when full; Father Tree (building) and Mother Mycelium (gifting/coordination); free frameworks; affiliate store at https://store.terrariumproject.org. It is deliberately not a rural commune and not bunker isolation. Suburban retrofit, urban/suburban permaculture, and cohousing grown inside existing neighborhoods are therefore a better fit than income-sharing land projects far from town.

**Fit (1–5)** is a Terrarium-relevance score, not a quality ranking:

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 5 | Close match: urban/suburban retrofit or neighborhood-scale resilience in or near California, with a public education or tour path. |
| 4 | Strong case study or California/neighborhood partner; some distance from the retrofit thesis, or a practitioner org rather than a live station. |
| 3 | Established, useful precedent (classic ecovillage, agrihood, or national educator) but rural, luxury-development, or only loosely aligned. |
| 2 | Notable, but philosophically or practically farther away (income-sharing communes, spiritual retreats, or thin public interface). |
| 1 | Forming, status uncertain, or include only as a watch item. |

Size figures are taken only from a page cited in the row. If a current official page does not state a headcount, the cell says so. Older directories (especially pre-2020 Coho/US and FIC lists) are not used as current population counts.

**Public contact route** is a website or contact page only. Personal emails and resident names found on HOA pages are omitted. No private individuals are named.

The July 2025 Terrarium Lords pitch deck line "Partnership with LA Eco-Village, 40+ members" is a game-scenario beat, not evidence of an existing real-world partnership. Luke's notes do name Los Angeles Eco-Village and The Emerald Village Observatory as preferred partners to approach.

## Top 15

These are the first places to study or, if Luke chooses, to approach. Rank is by Terrarium fit, not by fame.

### 1. Los Angeles Eco-Village (LAEV) — Los Angeles, CA — fit 5

Urban retrofit eco-village in a two-block, transit-rich neighborhood about three miles west of downtown. The intentional community is described as about 40 members inside a neighborhood of about 500 people, organized with the 45-unit Urban Soil / Tierra Urbana limited-equity housing cooperative and the Beverly-Vermont Community Land Trust. It began as a project of the nonprofit Cooperative Resources & Services Project after the 1992 civil unrest, with planning from 1993. Public tours, workshops, and short stays are offered. This is the closest working model of "reinvent how we live in the city" in Luke's own region, and it is already named in his notes as a preferred partner. Partnership angle: visit / tour first, then a case study and, only if they want it, a link exchange or workshop. Do not claim an existing partnership. Contact: https://laecovillage.org/ and the tours page at https://laecovillage.org/home/tours. Sources: https://laecovillage.org/ ; https://ustula.org/ ; https://ecovillage.org/map/community/los-angeles-eco-village/ ; https://www.ic.org/directory/community/los-angeles-eco-village/.

### 2. N Street Cohousing — Davis, CA — fit 5

The leading U.S. example of **retrofit cohousing**: it grew by connecting existing houses and opening back fences, rather than building a new compound. Official FAQ: 19 houses, about 50 residents, roughly six units per acre; private ownership, no income sharing, common meals and a common house. This is the clearest "station grown from existing suburbia" precedent in the country, and the scale sits inside Terrarium's 50–150 band. Partnership angle: arranged tour and case study (do not drop in). Contact: https://nstreetcohousing.org/ and https://nstreetcohousing.org/frequently-asked-questions/.

### 3. Village Homes — Davis, CA — fit 5

A 70-acre west-Davis subdivision designed for community and energy and resource conservation (official HOA site). UC Davis, covering the neighborhood's 50th anniversary, describes 245 residences, passive-solar houses, shared gardens, orchards, and bicycle-first streets. Designed by Michael and Judy Corbett; groundbreaking in the mid-1970s. It is the best long-running proof that ordinary suburban tract form can be rewritten without leaving town. Partnership angle: case study; public information is on the HOA about pages; any visit should go through the association, not residents. Contact: https://villagehomes.hoaspace.com/. Unit count source: https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/uc-davis-design-museum-showcases-innovative-neighborhood-50th-anniversary.

### 4. The Urban Homestead (Dervaes / Urban Homestead Institute) — Pasadena, CA — fit 5

A working urban homestead on a 1/5-acre (8,712 sq ft) lot, with a garden of about 1/10 acre, still operating as a public education site, farm-box program, and internship host. Official "Numbers" page (historical, not updated after visitor traffic changed the utility figures) records harvests rising from 2,300 lbs in 2001 to 7,030 lbs in 2010, plus greywater, solar, and a "giving back / grow community" practice. Jules Dervaes Jr. (1947–2016) is commemorated on the about page; Anaïs and Jordanne Dervaes now lead the Urban Homestead Institute. This is the compact suburban-lot proof for the first mile of the Suburban Gap thesis, in Luke's own metro. Partnership angle: visit a public tour or workshop; case study and content collaboration around household-scale production. Contact: https://www.urbanhomestead.org/ and https://www.urbanhomestead.org/about.

### 5. EcoVillage at Ithaca — Ithaca, NY — fit 5 for the "split when full" pattern

Three cohousing neighborhoods on one site, built in sequence: FROG (30 homes, 1997), SONG (30 homes, 2006), and TREE (40 households, completed 2015) — 100 homes total. TREE includes Passivhaus units and a four-story common house with living units. This is the cleanest documented example of a community reproducing by adding a new neighborhood when the first is full, which is exactly how Terrarium stations are supposed to split. Rural-adjacent, so it is a case-study partner more than a local one. Partnership angle: case study and content collab; check the site for current visit policy. Contact: https://ecovillageithaca.org/about/neighborhoods/.

### 6. The Emerald Village Observatory (EVO) — Vista, North County San Diego, CA — fit 4

Nine-acre intentional community founded in 2011 by ten members (FIC listing and community sites). The living community and a 501(c)(3), Emerald Village Oasis, share the land and host gatherings, workshops, and visits by request. Luke's July 2025 notes already name EVO as a preferred Southern California partner. It is more of a small land-based community and gathering place than a suburban-street retrofit, which is why it sits just under LAEV and N Street. Partnership angle: visit request via the Oasis form; relationship-building, not a public tour mill. Contact: https://theemeraldvillage.com/ ; visit form at https://www.emeraldvillageoasis.org/visit-request-form ; https://www.ic.org/directory/community/evo-the-emerald-village-in-vista-ca/.

### 7. Cool Petaluma — Petaluma, CA — fit 5 as a neighborhood-organizing model

Not an ecovillage. A grassroots nonprofit that organizes neighbors into Cool Teams on their own blocks for emergency preparedness, consumption, transportation, buildings, and nature. Official about page (fetched this pass): since launching in 2022 it has trained 273 team leaders, reached over 1,000 Petaluma households, and generated more than 6,000 individual climate actions. This is one of the closest living analogues to Terrarium's "start in existing suburbia, household by household and block by block" path. Partnership angle: case study and possible content or curriculum collaboration around block-scale preparedness. Contact: https://www.coolpetaluma.org/about.

### 8. Daily Acts — Petaluma, CA — fit 4

Petaluma nonprofit working on climate resilience, regenerative landscapes, education, and policy, with a mission (its "Our Story" page) of inspiring action that creates connected, equitable, climate-resilient communities. A practitioner organization, not a live-in station, but it already does suburban retrofit in the North Bay — gardens, stormwater, and neighborhood practice. Partnership angle: content collab, workshop cross-promotion, link exchange. Contact: https://dailyacts.org/contact/.

### 9. Occidental Arts & Ecology Center (OAEC) — Occidental, Sonoma County, CA — fit 4

An 80-acre research, demonstration, education, and community-organizing center. It trains civic and movement leaders to design regenerative systems at regional and local scale, hosts courses, tours, internships, and a biodiversity nursery, and works with schools, watershed groups, and public agencies. One of the strongest West Coast places to learn (and later teach) resilient community design without first buying land. Partnership angle: take a public course or tour; later a case study or curriculum relationship. Contact: https://oaec.org/.

### 10. Culdesac Tempe — Tempe, AZ — fit 4

A 17-acre, from-scratch walkable neighborhood next to a light-rail station. Official site: 700+ apartment homes, no resident parking, on-site retail, courtyards, e-bike and transit orientation, visitor/carshare parking only. It is a developer-built rental community, not a resident-governed station, but it is the highest-profile recent proof that a suburban arterial parcel can be rebuilt without cars. Partnership angle: case study (urban form, mobility, and "close the suburban gap" photography); leasing contacts are not a community partnership path. Contact: https://culdesac.com/tempe/.

### 11. The Cannery — Davis, CA — fit 4

Official HOA about page: a 98.6-acre mixed-use neighborhood that opened in 2016, 488 homes, more than 28 acres of open space, 9.9 miles of multi-use trails, community gardens, and a working urban farm. Marketed as Davis's first farm-to-table new housing community. A contemporary suburban agrihood in the same city as Village Homes, Muir Commons, and N Street — useful as a "what market-rate development will already fund" comparison. Partnership angle: case study; approach the association, not individual owners. Contact: https://www.canneryhoa.com/pages/about.

### 12. Muir Commons — Davis, CA — fit 4

The first newly built U.S. cohousing community modeled on the Danish pattern. Official site: groundbreaking November 1990, move-in summer 1991; 26 homes on just under three acres; about 45 adults and 35 children; common house, shop, garden, and orchard. Together with N Street and Village Homes, Davis is the densest cluster of suburban-community precedents in the country. Partnership angle: case study and an arranged visit if the community offers one. Contact: https://muircommons.org/.

### 13. The Ecology Center — San Juan Capistrano, CA — fit 4

Orange County public demonstration site for ecological living, food, and education (official site: https://theecologycenter.org/). Not a live-in village, but a Southern California suburban education node with tours and programs that already speak to households rather than to rural homesteaders. Partnership angle: visit, link exchange, content collab. Contact: https://theecologycenter.org/about/.

### 14. Transition Berkeley — Berkeley, CA — fit 4

Official about page: the 110th Transition U.S. initiative, launched at the Berkeley Ecology Center in February 2011, now a 501(c)(3). It organizes local food, repair, pollinator gardens, and neighbor skill-sharing as a response to climate change, fossil-fuel dependence, and economic instability. A city-scale mycelium, not a gated village. Partnership angle: content collab and mutual citation of free frameworks; Transition Streets is the household-by-household tool. Contact: https://www.transitionberkeley.org/about. National hub: Transition US, https://www.transitionus.org (Sebastopol-based 501(c)(3); directory at https://www.transitionus.org/directory/).

### 15. Eco Vista — Isla Vista, CA — fit 4, with an activity caveat

A Transition-style effort to treat the student community next to UC Santa Barbara as a transition town / eco-village project (housing, gardens, food forests, energy, community). Documented on the project's WordPress site and in a 20 April 2020 Resilience.org / Post Carbon Institute article, "Eco Vista: Building a Transition Town in a Student Community in California." This pass did not independently confirm 2025–2026 program intensity; treat current activity as something to verify before any approach. Geographic fit is unusually high (Isla Vista / UCSB). Partnership angle: case study first; only then a conversation, if the project is still active. Contact: https://ecovistacommunity.wordpress.com/ ; background: https://www.resilience.org/stories/2020-04-20/eco-vista-building-a-transition-town-in-a-student-community-in-california/.

## Stale, thin, or unverified listings (not counted above)

Older FIC / GEN-style lists (including a circulated "Ecovillages in the Fellowship of Intentional Communities Directory" PDF) name California projects that could not be confirmed as living, approachable communities in this pass. The others were not independently verified as current. Recheck via FIC and CalCoho before any use.
- **Resilient Design Institute** (Alex Wilson). A natural educator to include; secondary reporting says it closed after Wilson retired at the end of 2024. Not listed as an active partner.
- **Forming-only groups** beyond Coastal Cohousing (Ventura) and Southern Sonoma Cohousing were not added. The Alliance directory contains many "Forming" rows, some little more than a name.

## Suggested use (no outreach sent)

If Luke later wants a first wave of public, low-pressure approaches, a sensible order is: (1) read and visit LAEV's public tour, N Street (by appointment), Village Homes as a case study, and the Urban Homestead's public programs; (2) study Cool Petaluma, Daily Acts, Transition Berkeley / Transition US, and OAEC as organizing and education peers; (3) use EcoVillage at Ithaca as the written precedent for neighborhoods that split when full; (4) treat Culdesac, The Cannery, Agritopia, and Serenbe as built-form comparisons, not peer communities; (5) keep Earthaven and Olivette in the "public Asheville-region precedents" file. CalCoho and EBCOHO are better first doors to cohousing than emailing individual residents.
